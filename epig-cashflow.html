Ekantik Cash Flow is the EPIG 500 architecture with a withdrawal discipline on top: an 80% S&P foundation engineered to participate in only part of the market's declines, a 20% cash sleeve, and a buffer that funds the monthly draw — so the portfolio is never sold into weakness.

The withdrawal rule is 1.00% of NAV per month. Everything the architecture earns above that stays in the account and compounds, measured against the S&P.

The asymmetry is design intent, not a record. The buffer rule is arithmetic — and we publish the month it stops paying.

Income from Capital Comes in Two Flavors. Both Are Broken.

Safe and slow. Dividends, bonds, a 4% withdrawal rule. Predictable to the dollar, and so small that the freedom it buys arrives decades late — if it arrives.

Fast and lumpy. An edge that actually produces returns, paid out exactly as erratically as it earns them. Two strong months, one flat, one you'd rather not discuss. Real returns you cannot plan a single fixed expense around.

Almost everyone accepts one of these and quietly gives up the other. The assumption underneath — that you must choose between size and steadiness — is not a law of markets. It's an architecture problem. And architecture problems have solutions.

Stage 1 · Seasoning

3 months

The 10% buffer is funded at inception from the cash sleeve and held. Nothing is withdrawn for three months. Deterministic, not regime-dependent.

Stage 2 · Paying

1.00% / mo

Paid from the buffer only — never by selling the foundation. Gains in up months refill the buffer, capped so it never drains the sleeve faster than it earns.

Stage 3 · Suspension

Below 5%

If the buffer falls under 5% of NAV the withdrawal suspends automatically, and resumes only once it rebuilds to the full 10%. The rule protects the rule.

What This Costs You

1 · The withdrawal can suspend, and one day it will

A 10% buffer at 1% a month is ten months of coverage — not a guarantee. When it falls below 5% the payment stops until it rebuilds to 10%. In the 2000–2024 window with the engine off, the model suspends roughly a quarter of all eligible months. Suspension is designed behaviour, not a malfunction.

2 · 1% a month is a 11.4%/yr drag, and it needs the engine

Withdrawing 1% of NAV monthly compounds to an 11.4% annual withdrawal. The 80/40 foundation alone has historically produced roughly 9–10%/yr. The foundation alone does not fund this rate — the model shows capital eroding in real terms. On our runs the withdrawal only preserves inflation-adjusted capital once the engine clears about +8%/yr. Below that you may keep receiving every payment while your real capital shrinks. That is the failure mode you should watch for, and the model above will show it to you.

3 · The capture asymmetry has no verdict yet

80/40 blended capture is design intent under armed-but-unfired gates. The overlay has not completed a full market cycle. Its beat-or-protect gate is measured over a cycle and published when it resolves. Every capture figure on this page is an input to a model, not an observed result.

4 · The 20% cash sleeve is a real drag

Holding a fifth of the portfolio out of the market costs roughly 17% over a 20-year window versus 100% S&P, by EPIG's own disclosure. The overlay and the engine have to overcome that before they add anything. We publish the drag rather than net it away quietly.

5 · This is an income product, so it trades terminal wealth for cash now

Money withdrawn stops compounding. Over long windows a pure S&P holding ends with dramatically more capital than this architecture — because it never paid you anything along the way. If you do not need the monthly income, you should not buy an income product. Compare the NAV line to the S&P line in the model above and decide honestly.

If the 1% Isn't Paid, There Is No Fee

Not reduced. Not deferred. Not accrued and collected later. In any month the full 1% withdrawal does not reach you, no fee is charged for that month.

Why this is a real commitment, not a slogan. The suspension rule is mechanical and public — the buffer either cleared the floor that month or it did not. There is no discretion for us to exercise and no definition for us to renegotiate afterwards. The condition is observable by you, in the same month, from the same numbers.

In the model above, switching the fee on charges it only in paid months. Run a configuration that suspends often and watch the fee revenue collapse alongside the modelled withdrawals. That symmetry is the entire point: we are paid when the rule works, and not when it doesn't.

And today the fee is zero. Phase 1 carries no management fee at all — gross and net are identical, which is why the fee input defaults to 0.00%. When a schedule is published, this model deducts it and every figure updates. The field is live so you can stress-test our future pricing before we have set it.

The Hard Questions First

1% a month is 12% a year. Is that realistic?

Withdrawing 1% of NAV monthly is an 11.4%/yr drag once compounded. The 80/40 foundation on its own has historically returned about 9–10%/yr, so the foundation alone does not fund it — and the model will show you capital eroding if you turn the engine off. The rate is supportable in the model once the booster engine contributes roughly +8%/yr or better. That engine rate is a modelled haircut, not a promise, which is precisely why the model lets you set it to zero and see the consequence.

What happens the month the buffer runs out?

The withdrawal suspends automatically — no payment that month — and stays suspended until the buffer rebuilds to the full 10% of NAV, not merely back over the 5% floor. That hysteresis is deliberate: without it the model would oscillate pay/suspend/pay every month and produce a dishonest sawtooth. You are not charged a fee for any suspended month.

Why pay from a buffer instead of just selling shares?

Because selling shares to fund a withdrawal in a drawdown is the mechanism that turns a temporary decline into a permanent loss. The buffer exists so the foundation is never sold into weakness. The cost of that discipline is that the payment stops when the buffer is exhausted. We chose a visible interruption over invisible capital destruction.

Is the 80/40 capture asymmetry proven?

No. It is design intent with no verdict. The overlay has not completed a market cycle, and its beat-or-protect gate is measured across a full cycle and published when it resolves. The capture figures are model inputs. The historical S&P returns underneath them are real; what the overlay does to them is the untested part.

How is this different from the 4% rule?

The 4% rule draws from the portfolio itself, including in drawdowns, and is calibrated to survive rather than to pay well. This architecture targets roughly three times that rate, funds it from a dedicated buffer instead of by selling, dampens the drawdown it is exposed to — and, unlike the 4% rule, will visibly stop paying rather than quietly increase your odds of ruin.
